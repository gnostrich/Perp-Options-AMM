\begin{abstract}
Perpetual options let a trader cap liquidation loss without closing the position.
We show that a \emph{single} liquidity pool prices that protection at every strike
at once. The pool runs on the weighted constant-product (``Balancer'') curve, with
one structural change: each trade does not slide a point along a fixed curve but
\emph{re-leans} the curve, and that lean \emph{is} an option position. A single
static knob, set once from the asset's volatility, fixes the curve's steepness and
hence the pricing of the whole book of strikes. From one pool state we read the
value of every out-of-the-money call and put in closed form, with an exact
\emph{American} early-exercise level and a smooth join between waiting and
exercising. We state the conditions plainly. The early-exercise rule is proved
optimal over \emph{deterministic} exercise policies, and the stochastic
optimal-stopping identification is named, not formalised; exact replication holds
only per option ``wing'' for hedge ratio at most one; the curve and knob are
machine-checked by an external prover and \emph{trusted as compiled}, not re-run
here; and \emph{solvency stays conditional} on a funding source we show is
necessary but do not discharge. The geometry is necessary for solvency, never
sufficient.

\keywords{Automated market maker \and Decentralized finance \and Perpetual
options \and Optimal stopping \and Smooth pasting \and Formal verification}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and contributions}\label{sec:idea}
% ===========================================================================

An automated market maker (AMM) is a pool holding two assets whose quoted price is
the slope of a fixed curve, where a trade slides the reserve point along that
curve. Our pool starts from the same object---the weighted constant-product curve
of Balancer~\cite{balancer}---and makes one structural change: a trade does not
slide the point along the curve but \emph{re-leans the curve itself}. The pool
stores how the curve currently leans, and every trade updates that lean.
Figure~\ref{fig:warp} shows the construction: a trade lands at a point out along
the curve and re-leans it there.

That single change has one payoff: \emph{the lean of the curve is an option
position}. A pool whose curve leans a given way is, read correctly, holding (or
selling) a perpetual option at a particular strike, and because the curve covers a
range of slopes, one pool state prices an entire continuum of strikes---calls on
one side and puts on the other---at once and in closed form. There is no separate
premium and no expiry: the worth of a position is how far the curve has leaned,
read off as a number between zero and one.

One further parameter sets the curve's steepness. A \emph{shallower} curve prices a more
volatile asset---fatter wings make deep out-of-the-money strikes relatively richer
(via $\gamma(\gamma+1)=2r/\sigma^{2}$: higher volatility, lower $\gamma$). We fix the steepness once, at pool creation, with a single number
we call the \emph{vol knob}: the larger it is, the steeper the option-value curve
at \emph{every} strike, so the knob is calibrated to take a \emph{lower} setting for a
more volatile asset (shallower wings, richer tails). Its neutral setting recovers the plain Balancer curve. The
knob is set from the asset's volatility and never moves while trading; it is the
pool's geometry, not a quantity traders push around.

\paragraph{The result, stated first.}
The central positive result is that this single pool prices the entire
out-of-the-money strike continuum of \emph{American-style} perpetual options in
closed form, with an exact early-exercise level and a smooth join between waiting
and exercising. We state the conditions alongside it rather than burying them:
\emph{(i)}~``American'' means optimal over \emph{deterministic} exercise rules---a
genuine result---while the matching \emph{stochastic} optimal-stopping statement
is named but not proved; \emph{(ii)}~the construction replicates the target payoff
\emph{exactly} only per option wing, for positions whose price-sensitivity has
magnitude at most one (beyond that, a labelled approximation); \emph{(iii)}~the
boundary, the no-arbitrage symmetry, the curve, and the knob are checked by an
external theorem prover and \emph{trusted as compiled}, not independently re-run
here; and \emph{(iv)}~the pool's \emph{solvency is conditional} on a funding
source we prove is \emph{necessary} but do not discharge---the geometry alone is
never enough. Framed from the holder's side, the guarantee set is
\emph{American-consistency}: the holder may exercise at any time (settlement is
holder-invoked, never automatic); the quoted value is never below the exercised
payoff\textsuperscript{L6}; the exercised region pays the intrinsic (parity)
value by construction; and the join between waiting and exercising is
smooth\textsuperscript{L5} (superscripts index the machine-checked results in
Appendix~\ref{app:lean})---while the optimality statement of \emph{(i)} is
additional, exercise timing being the trader's own choice, not a system claim.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The curve family itself is \emph{not} new: as Section~\ref{sec:warp} shows, each
trade's arc is, after a change of coordinates, an ordinary constant product---a
translated market maker in the sense of Evans~\cite{evans2020} and the replication
theory of Angeris--Evans--Chitra~\cite{angeris-replicating}. What is new is:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item a \emph{per-trade weight update} that turns a Balancer pool into a
        state machine whose stored lean prices a tradable claim at every strike
        (Section~\ref{sec:warp});
  \item a \emph{semantic layer} over that pool---strike-as-a-ray, the mark, and
        settlement---that reads a continuum of option values from one pool state
        (Sections~\ref{sec:continuum},~\ref{sec:settlement});
  \item an \emph{American generalisation}: a closed-form smooth-pasting
        early-exercise boundary $S^{\ast}=K\,m\gamma/(m\gamma+1)$ (the base form
        $K\gamma/(\gamma+1)$ at $m=1$) --- with $K$ the dollar strike, $\gamma>1$
        the curve's \emph{convexity exponent} (larger $\gamma$ = a steeper
        option-value curve), and $m$ the steepness knob below --- with a smooth
        (value-and-slope) join and no new parameters, tied to the classical
        perpetual-American (Merton) problem (Section~\ref{sec:american});
  \item a single static, \emph{volatility-calibrated} curvature knob $m$ that
        sets the option-value steepness uniformly across strikes
        (Section~\ref{sec:lens});
\end{enumerate}
together with an honest formal-verification ledger (Section~\ref{sec:frontier})
that states exactly what is machine-checked, under what label, and where the open
frontier is---namely that solvency is conditional on a funding port shown
necessary but not discharged.

\subsection{The Balancer curve}
A two-asset constant-function market maker is a curve in the plane of reserves,
$x$ of the underlying asset and $y$ of cash. The pool's state is a single point on
that curve, and the price it quotes is the slope of the curve at that point. The
Balancer curve is
\begin{equation}
  x^{w}\,y^{1-w}=k ,
  \label{eq:balancer}
\end{equation}
read as: \emph{the weighted geometric mean of the two reserves stays at a fixed
level}. Two numbers describe it. The \emph{weight} $w\in(0,1)$ says how the curve
\emph{leans} between the two assets---$w=\tfrac12$ is the symmetric, equal-weight
curve, and pushing $w$ away from $\tfrac12$ tilts pricing toward one asset. The
\emph{level} $k$ says how far out the curve sits---its overall scale. In ordinary
use $w$ and $k$ are fixed and trades slide the point $(x,y)$ along the curve.

\subsection{Trades bend the curve}
Our one structural change is that \emph{a trade updates the weight $w$}, and a
change in $w$ re-leans the curve~\eqref{eq:balancer}---it warps. The pool becomes
a \emph{state-transition system}: a sequence of state updates, one per trade, not
a single fixed equation. The stored state is the triple $(x,y,w)$, with $w$
genuine memory; the level $k=x^{w}y^{1-w}$ is a dependent \emph{readout} that
trades do not hold constant.

Two views of a trade coincide. Sliding the origin to \emph{shifted virtual
reserves} $u=x-\alpha$ and $v=y-\beta$, where $\alpha=xw$ and $\beta=y(1-w)$ are
curve offsets, each individual trade's arc satisfies the ordinary constant product
$u\,v=\text{const}$. So the per-trade geometry is nothing exotic: it is a
\emph{translated} constant-function market maker in the sense of
Evans~\cite{evans2020}, and the ``bending'' picture is a re-description of a weight
change, not a new invariant. \textbf{This is exactly why we do not claim the curve
family as a contribution}---we claim the per-trade weight \emph{update} and the
semantic layer built on it.

\subsection{Trades happen anywhere on the curve}
In ordinary market makers a trade is always at the reserve point. Here a trade may
land at \emph{any} out-of-the-money point on the curve. Each ray from the
origin---a line $y=\theta x$ of fixed reserve ratio, with slope $\theta$---is
identified with one strike. The ray meets the curve at a single \emph{trade
point}, and a transaction there is handled as if that point were the reserve
point: the conservation law is applied locally, at the trade's own point. The
$45^{\circ}$ ray ($\theta=1$) is the at-the-money strike; steeper and shallower
rays split the continuum into the two \emph{wings}---the call side and the put
side---both priced from the same $(x,y,w)$ state (Figure~\ref{fig:warp}).

Explicitly, write $T=(x_{T},y_{T})$ for the trade point on the ray, and
$\alpha_{T}=x_{T}\,w$, $\beta_{T}=y_{T}\,(1-w)$ for the curve offsets of
Section~\ref{sec:warp} evaluated \emph{at $T$}. A trade whose cash leg is
$\Delta y$ (the cash the trader pays in or takes out) updates the state
$(x,y,w)$ by

% From (x,y,w)=(10,10,1/2): curve x^{1/2}y^{1/2}=10, i.e. xy=100; the ray

\begin{equation}
  y'=y+\Delta y,\qquad
  x'=x+\Delta x,\quad
  \Delta x=-\frac{\alpha_{T}\,\beta_{T}\,\Delta y}
                 {(y_{T}-\beta_{T})\,(y_{T}+\Delta y-\beta_{T})},\qquad
  w'=\frac{\alpha_{T}}{x_{T}+\Delta x},
  \label{eq:tradeupdate}
\end{equation}
on the admissible domain $y_{T}+\Delta y>\beta_{T}$ and subject to reserve
feasibility ($x+\Delta x>0$, $y+\Delta y>0$). In words: the reserve flows
$\Delta x,\Delta y$ are the ordinary translated-constant-product amounts
computed at the trade's own point $T$ rather than at the reserve point (they
conserve the local pair $\alpha_{T},\beta_{T}$), the flows are drawn from the
pool's actual reserves $(x,y)$, and the new lean $w'$ is read off at the
displaced trade point $x_{T}+\Delta x$.

A spot swap is the special case where the trade point \emph{is} the reserve point;
there, and only there, the trade traces the familiar constant-product trajectory.
Off-the-money trades genuinely change the global offsets $\alpha,\beta$ by design;
nothing is violated, because those were never claimed constant across such trades.
That $w$ must be stored (rather than recomputed from $x$) is shown by a small
arithmetic exhibit of~\eqref{eq:tradeupdate}: from $(x,y,w)=(10,10,\tfrac12)$
the $\theta=4$ trade point is $T=(5,20)$, and a unit cash leg $\Delta y=1$
gives $\Delta x=-5/22$ and $w'=11/21$---not the global recompute
$\alpha/x'=22/43$ (pre-trade $\alpha=xw$ over the new reserve $x'$).

\subsection{What machine-checking covers here}
The conservation-at-the-trade-point rule, the shifted-reserve hyperbola, and the
weight update rest on a machine-checked spine---the pool invariant and trade
law\textsuperscript{L1} and the trade/rebase
commutation\textsuperscript{L2} (\tfp{}, Section~\ref{sec:frontier}). The
off-the-money trade-point existence/uniqueness lemmas have been submitted to the
prover and report complete, but are not yet folded to our \tfp{} bar; we do not
count them among the machine-checked results.

\section{Options across the strike continuum}\label{sec:continuum}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{The perpetual option}
A perpetual option is a never-expiring claim on favourable price action---upward
for a call, downward for a put---of the underlying past a strike $K$. Composed
with a perpetual future, it caps the trader's loss beyond $K$ without forcing the
position closed, and because it never expires the protection persists for the life
of the perp. The only decision the holder faces is \emph{when} to realise it.

\subsection{The mark: value as a fraction, no separate premium}
The worth of a position is its \emph{mark}: the smooth-pasted American value of
Section~\ref{sec:american}, a fraction in $(0,1]$ of the wing's \emph{escrow
unit} (one full perpetual future). Out-of-the-money the mark is the
\emph{continuation} (waiting) value; past the \emph{free boundary} (the
optimal-exercise level) it is the exercised (parity) value; it reaches $1$
only at \emph{full} exercise---at the money it is the continuation value
($0.148$ at $\gamma=2$ in the worked example of Section~\ref{sec:american}).
The ingredient is still a \emph{ray read}: moneyness is the strike ray's slope
read against the pool's own ray---one ray, one curve---and the waiting value
is a power of that read times a constant fixed at the smooth join. A position
of size $q$ (its \emph{notional}) is worth $q\cdot\mathrm{mark}$, and nothing
else---no separate premium object, no expiry, no intrinsic/extrinsic split;
the whole continuum is priced from the one pool state $(x,y,w)$.

\subsection{The reference price (``carry'')}
The pool keeps an internal reference price---a price it treats as its own
centre---which we call the \emph{carry} and write
\begin{equation}
  P=N_{y}/N_{x},
\end{equation}
the ratio of the pool's notional cash holding $N_{y}$ to its notional underlying
holding $N_{x}$. A strike is registered relative to the carry by the log-distance
$u=\log(\text{price})-\log P$ (how far, in log-price, a price sits from the
centre). To keep the pool's price from drifting away from the external oracle
price, \emph{strike normalisation} periodically \emph{rebases} the frame:
rescaling coordinates by a single factor $r>0$ (so $x\to rx$, the offset
$\alpha\to r\alpha$, the level $k\to r^{w}k$, and each strike ray
$\theta\to\theta/r$). Trades and rebases \emph{commute}, so the pricing frame is
well defined regardless of their order (machine-checked: \tfp{}\textsuperscript{L2}).

\paragraph{Funding.}
Mirroring a perpetual future, a position accrues \emph{funding} at a rate
proportional to how far the live curve deviates from the symmetric ($w=\tfrac12$)
anchor curve, measured at the position's own strike ray and read through the vol
knob of Section~\ref{sec:lens}. The crowded side pays the contrarian side, and a
rebase bounds how fast drift accumulates into that deviation. With a vol-free
mark this funding vanishes exactly at the anchor; the knob is what gives it a
vol-aware scale. We do not claim it neutralises any specific equilibrium drift;
that analysis is deferred (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}).

The pool above prices within one fixed liquidity shape; the remaining degree of
freedom is the shape's steepness. Steepness encodes how concentrated value is near
the money, which is a volatility choice. We realise it as a \emph{lens}: a single
read-time transformation---a lens because every priced quantity is read
\emph{through} it, as through a fixed optical element---that rescales the curve's
steepness by one number without touching the pool's reserves or trade mechanics.

\subsection{Definition}
Let $\gamma>1$ be the curve's \emph{convexity exponent}: the steepness parameter
for which option value follows the power law $\mathrm{value}\propto S^{-\gamma}$
far from the strike (in the wings), with $S$ the spot price. The larger $\gamma$,
the steeper the curve. The lens adds one scalar $m>0$ and sets the \emph{lensed}
steepness
\begin{equation}
  g_{\mathrm{loc}}(K)=m\,\gamma\qquad\text{for every strike }K,
  \label{eq:lens}
\end{equation}
in words: \emph{the steepness is multiplied by $m$, equally at every strike}.
Setting $m=1$ gives back the plain Balancer steepness $\gamma$; larger $m$ makes
the option-value curve steeper everywhere (Figure~\ref{fig:lens}), and sends a
trade further out along the curve through the frozen map
$\theta_{\mathrm{tx}}=\mathrm{mode}\cdot(\mathrm{chosen}/\mathrm{mode})^{m}$,
where $\mathrm{chosen}$ is the ray the trader selected, $\mathrm{mode}$ the
at-the-money centre, and $\theta_{\mathrm{tx}}$ the ray the trade actually lands
on. Steepness and outward placement move in the \emph{same} direction with $m$.

\subsection{What the lens is and is not}
The lens is \emph{vol-calibrated at setup and static under trading}: it is the
curve's geometry chosen to suit an asset of a given volatility (a more volatile
asset takes a \emph{lower} setting---shallower wings, fatter tails;
Section~\ref{sec:idea}), not a trader-measured statistic. It leaves the \textbf{pool flow unchanged}---the trade
rule, the oracle-arbitrage map, and the rebase are the plain Balancer dynamics;
$x,y,w$ move exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:warp}. It is applied as a forward read
to everything \emph{read} (pricing, the chart, settlement, funding, portfolio
value) and \emph{written} (trades, settle-at-strike), is never inverted, and the
underlying pool stays plain Balancer. The pool-unchanged and
$g_{\mathrm{loc}}=m\gamma$ facts are machine-checked (\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L3}).

\paragraph{The pricing ceiling: level and skew, not smile curvature.}
We are explicit about how much of an option surface this can fit. The cross-strike
surface is governed by essentially \emph{two} shape numbers---the tail steepness
$m\gamma$ and the skew $w$---giving one power-law exponent per wing. It can match a
surface's \emph{level} and \emph{skew} but not strike-dependent \emph{smile
curvature}, since one exponent per wing cannot bend differently across strikes.
This is the same object as the single-pool rigidity that buys the closed form
(Section~\ref{sec:frontier}). Whether a single-exponent tail is adequate for a
given real asset is an \emph{empirical} question; a backtest would speak only to
tail adequacy and would not discharge the carried Merton or distributional
hypotheses. For the out-of-the-money liquidation strikes the design targets---which
are level- and skew-dominated---we expect the smile-curvature ceiling to be
second-order, but we state this as a plausibility, not a proof.

This is the paper's core construction. In the prior treatment the position was a
\emph{barrier}: its mark saturated to $1$ at the strike and stayed at that ceiling.
Here we lift the
ceiling into an American-style \emph{early-exercise free boundary}---a price level,
not fixed in advance but solved for, marking where the holder optimally exercises.

\subsection{The construction}
While the holder waits, the position is worth its \emph{continuation value}
$c\cdot s_{N}$, where $s_{N}$ is the \emph{normalised strike coordinate} (the
strike in the curve's own scaled units, with value $\propto s_{N}\propto
S^{-\gamma}$) and $c$ fixes the overall level. Continuation \emph{pastes} onto the
exercised (intrinsic) payoff at the boundary under \emph{smooth-pasting}: the two
pieces must agree in both value and slope---two equations in the two unknowns
$s_{N}^{\ast}$ and $c$. The solution is closed form:
\begin{equation}
  S^{\ast}=K\,\frac{g_{\mathrm{loc}}}{g_{\mathrm{loc}}+1}
         =K\,\frac{m\gamma}{m\gamma+1},
  \qquad
  \text{value fraction at the boundary}=\frac{1}{g_{\mathrm{loc}}+1},
  \label{eq:boundary}
\end{equation}
where $g_{\mathrm{loc}}=m\gamma$ is the lensed (through-the-knob) steepness. The
boundary therefore \emph{moves with the lens}; it reduces to the base form
$S^{\ast}=K\gamma/(\gamma+1)$ only in the plain-Balancer special case $m=1$.
The mark reaches its cap of $1$ only at \emph{full} exercise, not at the boundary
(Figure~\ref{fig:seam}). Here $S^{\ast}$ is the \emph{optimal-exercise} (pricing)
boundary, not a forced trigger: exercise is the holder's right at any time, and
$S^{\ast}$ is merely where a rational holder would exercise and what the valuation
assumes. On the engine, settlement is a trader-invoked close, never an auto-exercise.
With that reading, the exercise switch fires exactly where the ratio
intrinsic/continuation hits $1$, which is $S^{\ast}$---past the strike, not at the
money. The continuation runs \emph{through} the old strike ray, so the strike
becomes an ordinary interior point; on the bounded (spread) wing the intrinsic
$1-S/K$ caps at $1$, while a single uncapped option wing keeps growing past $1$,
as it should.

\subsection{A worked example (illustrative numbers)}
We evaluate the formulas at \emph{illustrative} values, chosen for arithmetic
clarity, not calibrated to any market and not a validation of anything. Take a put leg with $\gamma=2$, $K=\$100$, $m=1$ (plain Balancer, so
$g_{\mathrm{loc}}=m\gamma=2$). The boundary~\eqref{eq:boundary} sits at
$S^{\ast}=100\cdot\tfrac23=\$66.67$, below the strike, with mark
$1/(g_{\mathrm{loc}}+1)=1/3$ there. Steepening to $m=3$ ($g_{\mathrm{loc}}=6$)
\emph{moves} the boundary, because $C^{1}$ smooth-pasting forces the boundary and
the continuation arm to share the \emph{same} exponent $g_{\mathrm{loc}}$ (a
mismatched pair would kink the seam for $m\neq1$). The boundary rises to
$S^{\ast}=100\cdot\tfrac{6}{7}=\$85.71$ with boundary mark
$1/(g_{\mathrm{loc}}+1)=1/7\approx0.143$, and below $S^{\ast}$ the leg is simply
intrinsic, $1-S/K$. The off-boundary continuation mark also decays faster, via the
wing power law $\mathrm{value}\propto S^{-g_{\mathrm{loc}}}$. Each column below is
evaluated at \emph{its own} boundary:

\subsection{Why this is the American boundary, and the Merton tie}
The smooth-paste solution is not an arbitrary $C^{1}$ join. It is the \emph{unique}
solution of the value-and-slope system at any steepness $g$---hence at the lensed
$g_{\mathrm{loc}}=m\gamma$ (machine-checked for generic $g$;
\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L4}). The pasted object of Figure~\ref{fig:seam}
itself---the power continuation welded onto the \emph{linear} exercised payoff in
the dollar frame---is machine-checked as well (\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L5,L6}):
value and slope match at $S^{\ast}$ for every steepness $g>0$; the welded value
function is differentiable \emph{at} the seam, not merely arm-by-arm; the
two-equation value-and-slope system \emph{forces} $S^{\ast}=K\,g/(g+1)$ and the
continuation level uniquely; and the quoted value is never below the exercised
payoff, strictly above it beyond the boundary. (These are statements about the
put-wing pricing object depicted; the call-wing value-and-slope match is also
checked.) The boundary moreover \emph{maximises} the holder's value over
\emph{deterministic} exercise boundaries (a genuine variational result;
\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L7}). The identification with the \emph{stochastic}
optimal-stopping (Snell) boundary is \emph{named but not formalised}---a placeholder
obligation, not a discharged premise.

The boundary also sits on a tie to the classical perpetual-American (Merton)
problem. Here $r$ is the risk-free interest rate and $\sigma$ the underlying's
return volatility (this $r$ is distinct from the rebase factor). The exponent
$\gamma$ is the \emph{characteristic root} of the underlying's log-price process.
Concretely, the roots in play are those of the \emph{zero-net-carry} pricing ODE
$\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}S^{2}V''(S)-rV(S)=0$, where $V(S)$ is the option value as a
function of spot---the absent first-order ($SV'$) term \emph{is} the symmetric
pairing, distinguishing it from the standard no-dividend
Black--Scholes--Merton ODE; substituting a power law $V=S^{\rho}$ gives
$\rho(\rho-1)=2r/\sigma^{2}$, from which~\eqref{eq:merton} can be re-derived.
Under the \emph{symmetric pairing} we use---a put root $-\gamma$ and a call root
$\gamma+1$, equivalently zero net carry between the legs, so the two roots sum to
$1$---the product-of-roots (Vieta) relation reads
\begin{equation}
  \gamma(\gamma+1)=2r/\sigma^{2},
  \qquad\text{in the regime }r>\sigma^{2},
  \label{eq:merton}
\end{equation}
the convention encoded by our machine-checked Vieta sum/product
relations\textsuperscript{L8}.
(The classical no-dividend Black--Scholes--Merton slice instead keeps the single
decaying root and gives the \emph{linear} relation $\gamma=2r/\sigma^{2}$; we do
not use that slice here.) On the symmetric pairing $S^{\ast}$ is the Merton
boundary, with the Gaussian-slice limit machine-checked
(\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L8}) and the generalized-hyperbolic / Bessel-K distributional layer carried as
named hypotheses---exact on the log-normal (Gaussian) slice, conditional beyond it.
This distributional tie is stated at the base characteristic root $\gamma$ (the
unlensed $m=1$ slice); the lens then transports the \emph{engine} boundary to
$g_{\mathrm{loc}}=m\gamma$ by the same generic-$g$ uniqueness above.

\paragraph{How literally to read the Merton relation.}
Taken literally as a calibration formula, \eqref{eq:merton} lives in the regime
$r>\sigma^{2}$, which fails at crypto-scale volatility: at $\sigma\approx80\%$
it would demand a risk-free rate above $64\%$. We therefore use the relation as
a \emph{theory anchor}: it fixes the \emph{direction} of calibration---higher
volatility, lower $\gamma$, a shallower curve with fatter wings
(Section~\ref{sec:idea})---and it is exact on the Gaussian slice, but $\gamma$
and the knob $m$ are \emph{design parameters} chosen at deployment, not
quantities implied from market $r$ and $\sigma$ through the formula.

\subsection{The seam, checked two ways}
At the boundary the value-match and slope-match hold for every steepness $g>0$,
hence at $g=m\gamma$---machine-checked both in the curve's own
coordinate\textsuperscript{L4} and, for the welded linear seam of
Figure~\ref{fig:seam}, in the dollar frame\textsuperscript{L5} (\tfp{})---and
the out-of-the-money$\to$in-the-money crossover lands at the dollar strike $K$ for
all $\gamma$ (\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L4}). On the live engine, the shipped
implementation reproduces the worked-example table above cell-for-cell to four
decimal places, read off the rendered interface---both the $m=1$ and $m=3$
columns, on both the continuation and exercised arms, and at the two boundary
points ($\$66.67$ and $\$85.71$)---and the value-never-below-exercised-payoff
property is enforced as a hard engine gate. These are engine measurements, not
Lean theorems.

\section{Properties: no internal arbitrage, settlement, replication}
\label{sec:props}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{No internal arbitrage (the costless collar)}
The sharp statement concerns a \emph{costless collar}: a band whose sold and
bought legs are cash-neutral by construction. We do \emph{not} claim that no
composition of swaps yields a riskless edge---no formal result covers swap
compositions, and that claim is withdrawn until one does. What we establish is a
clean symmetry.

\begin{proposition}[Collar surplus vanishes exactly at symmetry]\label{prop:collar}
The costless-collar capital-efficiency surplus is zero \emph{if and only if} the
pool is symmetric, $w=\tfrac12$. Under skew ($w\neq\tfrac12$) the surplus is
non-zero, with an explicit counterexample.
\end{proposition}

The biconditional is the substance, stated at its true strength. The formalised
surplus is a \emph{posited} form proportional to anchor asymmetry,
so the result is a symmetry statement about that posited surplus, not a
mechanism-level no-arbitrage theorem over swap compositions; we carry it as a named
definition with the skew sign exhibited (\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L9}). A
companion result casts the symmetry as a reflection---the put at
coordinate $s$ is the call reflected through the at-the-money ray
($s\mapsto\theta^{2}/s$)---so no-arbitrage \emph{is} the curve's symmetry
(\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L9}). We are precise: the reflection arrow is a
proved identity, but the link from the spec mark to the live engine's instrument is
a residual modeling premise, so this is best read as a \emph{conditional skeleton},
not an unconditional no-arbitrage theorem over the production engine.

A costless collar is not free of \emph{pool motion}---its two legs move $w$---but
the trader pays for that displacement at fair, sequenced prices (a swap's proceeds
are the path integral of the marginal price), so there is no free cash. Zero skew
removes the \emph{free} edge, not the \emph{worth} of the protection.

\subsection{Settlement}\label{sec:settlement}
A position is opened as a \emph{band}---a composition of legs---against a perpetual
future the trader already holds, the \emph{origin perp}. At open the band is minted
against a carved slice of that perp, whose notional, equity, and entry mark are
frozen for the band's life, so settlement attribution cannot be contaminated by
unrelated perp activity. Settlement is therefore \emph{perp-denominated}: leg
values are carried in units of the carved perp and converted to cash at closing
equity. A band has two legs on opposite wings; since spot cannot be in-the-money on
both at once, at most one leg is in-the-money at any time, giving a clean two-case
settlement. The composite-ray closed form holds across the
out-of-the-money$\to$in-the-money boundary under the effective-strike substitution
(machine-checked: \tfp{}\textsuperscript{L10}).

\subsection{Replication scope: $|\Gamma|\le1$ exact, $|\Gamma|>1$ approximate}
Let $\Gamma$ be the \emph{hedge ratio}---a position's price-sensitivity to spot.
The construction replicates the target American payoff \emph{exactly} for
$|\Gamma|\le1$, a bound that is \emph{per wing}: each wing manufactures a delta of
magnitude at most one. The shipped instrument is a vanilla \emph{opposite-wing
band}---two legs on opposite wings, at most one in-the-money at a time---so the
active wing is always a single $|\Gamma|\le1$ leg, and replication is exact for the
shipped product. The $|\Gamma|>1$ regime arises only outside that design
(same-wing stacking or single-leg leverage), where ``true American'' exercise and
``exact replication per wing'' cannot both hold; we scope it out of the product but
keep it \emph{disclosed} as a labelled approximation (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}).

\subsection{Provenance: \tfp{}, never ``verified''}

provenance map, are in the anonymized artifact repository (available on request),

hypothesis) from \emph{carried} results (conditional on named, undischarged
structure-field hypotheses---never on \texttt{axiom}s); the full formal-artifact
map and Lean statements are available on request in the anonymized artifact
repository.

\subsection{Solvency is conditional---the headline open item}
The construction's solvency guarantee is \emph{conditional}. A separate prover line
formalises the curve\,$\to$\,value\,$\to$\,storage interface as a typed stack and
proves, machine-checked\textsuperscript{L11}: pool value is concave in price for
any valid curve and this propagates into stored equity by types; and the headline
\texttt{reserves\_have\_no\_floor}\textsuperscript{L12}---with a convex
obligation, reserve value is
unbounded below, so \emph{the solvency floor cannot come from reserves}
(``convexity must be funded'' as a theorem). It then proves: \emph{given} a port
that covers the concave deficit, the pool is passive and solvent for all time with
no free lunch.

That ``given'' is the whole point. The hypotheses---B1 (a funding port covers the
floor), B3 (arbitrage is one-way), and B4 (the settlement ledger closes)---are
carried as \emph{structure fields}, not discharged facts. The stack proves
``\emph{if} the ledger closes, arbitrage is one-way, and the port covers the floor,
\emph{then} passivity and solvency''; it does \emph{not} prove the port pays in the
real world. Geometry is shown \emph{necessary} (the no-floor
theorem\textsuperscript{L12}) but never \emph{sufficient}. These are
\emph{assumptions, not claims}, and the reader should not mistake conditional
solvency for an unconditional result. The proven instances are the equal-weight
constant-product pool and an exponential pool\textsuperscript{L11}; the typed
contracts are
\emph{not} yet wired to the production engine's numeric formulas---that
instantiation is the next body of work.

\subsection{The whole exchange as a single object (research direction)}
We have a partial result toward unifying the entire exchange---storage, flow,
funding port, settlement, dollar pipe---as one passive object whose solvency reads
as passivity-under-admissible-inputs. The \emph{internal} half is machine-checked
(\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L13}): the system is passive because the geometry is
positive-semidefinite. The
\emph{external}/solvency half stays \emph{open}, keeping the coverage premise
undischarged and honoring the no-floor theorem\textsuperscript{L12}. We therefore present
the unified single object as a \emph{conjecture / research direction}, not a
finished proven artifact.

\section{Related work, limitations, conclusion}\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Related work.}
The weighted constant-product invariant is from Balancer~\cite{balancer}, whose
Liquidity Bootstrapping Pools already use time-varying weights; our per-trade
update is a finer-grained, trade-driven analogue, not a new curve family. CFMM
pricing-oracle theory is from Angeris et al.~\cite{angeris-cfmm,angeris-uniswap},
with Uniswap v2/v3 as references~\cite{uniswapv2,uniswapv3}. That each trade is,
under shifted virtual reserves, an ordinary constant product is the
translated-curve view of Evans~\cite{evans2020}; the replication theory of
Angeris--Evans--Chitra~\cite{angeris-replicating}, instantiated in
RMM-01~\cite{rmm01}, chooses a trading function to replicate a target
payoff---so we do not claim the curve family as a contribution. InfinityPools~%
\cite{infinitypools} builds options-like exposure on concentrated liquidity, and
Panoptic~\cite{panoptic} is the closest oracle-free perpetual-options protocol; we
differ in pricing the whole continuum from one pool state and in the American free
boundary. QuantAMM's weight-dynamic AMM work~\cite{quantamm} is \emph{prior} (not
concurrent); we use the neutral ``per-trade weight-dynamic AMM'' descriptor. The Lean-AMM line is
anchored by Pusceddu--Bartoletti~\cite{pusceddu-bartoletti}; our ledger extends it
to the smooth-pasting boundary, the lens, and the conditional-solvency stack, on
Lean~4~\cite{lean4} and Mathlib~\cite{mathlib}. The nearest analytic neighbour is
loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR) of Milionis et al.~\cite{lvr}: an LP position read as
a continuum of short perpetual options is the \emph{dual} of our sell-side
construction, and we reuse the LVR framing for the small \emph{round-trip residual}
of our transition rule (opening then reversing at the same ray leaves a small
trader-borne cost, a path-dependence of the class in Curve~v2; a quantitative
treatment is deferred).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Beyond the headline conditional solvency, we carry as honest open surfaces:
exact replication is per-wing $|\Gamma|\le1$ ($|\Gamma|>1$ a labelled
approximation); single-pool rigidity fixes the liquidity shape (level and skew,
not smile curvature); the Merton tie is exact only on the Gaussian slice, with the
generalized-hyperbolic / Bessel-K layer carried, not discharged; the
trusted-from-prover interface is not yet wired to the production engine (the
bridge is an oracle, not a verified extraction); the carved-perp settlement ledger,
the funding equilibrium-drift quantification, the round-trip residual, and the
notional-to-cash-leg sizing across the bought leg are open; and the prior-art
citation identifiers added in revision will be hand-checked against primary
sources before submission. None of these is dropped; each is stated rather than
buried.

\paragraph{Mechanism design: open.}
This paper's contribution is the pricing construction and its machine-checked
spine, not an equilibrium analysis. Incentive compatibility of the funding
schedule, the behaviour of strategic traders near the exercise boundary
$S^{\ast}$ and around the static knob $m$, the participation rationality of
liquidity providers, and a welfare comparison against order-book alternatives
are explicitly open, and we name them as future work. They are where the formal
spine meets the market: the conditional-solvency hypotheses of
Section~\ref{sec:frontier} (a funding port that pays, one-way arbitrage, a
ledger that closes) are behavioural assumptions, and discharging them is a
mechanism-design task as much as a proof task.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
A single Balancer pool with one dynamic weight---run as a state-transition system
whose conservation law is applied at each trade's own point, and whose trades
\emph{bend} the curve---prices the entire out-of-the-money perpetual-option
continuum in closed form. We lift the prior barrier into an American-style
perpetual option: a closed-form smooth-pasting boundary $S^{\ast}=K\,m\gamma/(m\gamma+1)$
(the base form $K\gamma/(\gamma+1)$ at $m=1$)
with a smooth seam and no new parameters, machine-checked
(\tfp{}\textsuperscript{L4,L5,L6}) and tied to the
Merton problem, plus a constant slope-multiplier lens $m$ that acts as a
vol-calibrated knob while leaving the pool flow unchanged. On internal arbitrage we
claim only what is proved: a posited collar surplus vanishes iff the pool is
symmetric. The honest frontier is solvency: geometry is necessary
(the no-floor theorem\textsuperscript{L12}) but never sufficient, and solvency stays
\emph{conditional} on a funding port shown necessary but not discharged. Closing
that port hypothesis and wiring the trusted-from-prover interface to the production
engine are the principal directions for future work.
